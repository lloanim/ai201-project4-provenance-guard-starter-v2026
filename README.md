# Provenance Guard
---

## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->
Provenance Guard is a service that estimates whether a piece of writing was produced by a human or by AI. A submission moves through these steps:

1. **Submit** — A client sends plain-English text to the service as a JSON request.
2. **Model signal** — A model scores the text based on its *meaning* (what it says).
3. **Style signal** — A second scorer rates the text based on its *shape* (how it is written: sentence length, structure, word patterns).
4. **Combine** — The two scores are merged into a single confidence number.
5. **Label** — That number maps to one of three labels a reader sees: `high-confidence human`, `unsure`, or `high-confidence AI`.
6. **Audit log** — Every decision is recorded with its `content_id`, both signal scores, the combined score, and the label, so it can be reviewed later.
7. **Appeal** — If someone disagrees with a label, they submit the `content_id` along with their reasoning, and the content's status changes to `under_review`.

## The Shape
For the capstone, I chose **Shape C: extending one existing capability**, plus **Path B** for logging. My system wrongly accuses careful, formal human writers. Formal test samples FP03–FP06 are labeled AI, and my own essays clear the AI line by only 0.003. To fix that, I'm adding a **formal-register check** to the existing `scoring.py` flow. The check flags text as formal when it has transition phrases like "furthermore" and "it is important to," few contractions, and no first-person or casual words. Both signals still run exactly as they do now. When the text is flagged formal, a `high-confidence AI` label is capped at `unsure`, and the response says why. The cost is that formal AI text gets capped too, so I'll rerun `run_eval.py` and `run_attacks.py` before and after and report how many real AI texts drop from AI to unsure. For Path B, each decision's log entry will record latency, tokens, memory, outcome, and a new `formal_flag` field.

**Out of Scope:**
- **I'm not rebuilding either signal.** The model signal and the style signal keep the structure and measures they have now. The formal check sits on top of them.
- **I'm not retuning the signal weights or the label thresholds.** The fix comes from the formal-register cap, not from moving numbers until the tests pass. (I did move the style ranges and weights out of `stylometry.py` into `config.py` so they're easier to see, but their values didn't change.)
- **I'm not adding a third scoring signal.** The formal check can only lower a label to `unsure`. It never adds to the AI score.

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:**
     It measures how surprising each next word (token) is to a local language model. It measures by perplexity where low means the model easily predicted the text and high means the text kept surprising it.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:**
     It differs because AI text is generated by picking high probability words, so another model would find it predictable, giving low perplexity. Humans make odd word choices, use slang and idioms, and change direction mid-sentence, which makes their text more surprising. 

**What it can't see:**
     It can't establish authorship by just simply measuring how predictable the writing is. Predictable writing does not automatically mean it's AI-generated, and unpredictable writing does not automatically mean it's original human writing. For example, formulaic human writing like lab reports can score low and get flagged as AI. AI text can be made less predictable by prompting it to write a certain way or a human swapping some words. 

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:**
     It measures the shape of the text, not its meaning. So it measures how much the sentence lengths vary (sentence_length_spread), how varied the vocabulary is (type_token_ratio), and how much punctuation is used (punctuation_density).

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:**
     It differs because AI writing tends to be more even with its sentences. It has similar lengths, predictable words, and regular punctuation. Human writing is lumpier with short and long sentences mixed together, fragments, and repeated favorite words. 

**What it can't see:**
     It only sees surface form, so surface edits fool it. It also has the issue where AI-generated writing can be told to write in a more human-like way and pass. Or a human can write more formally, which makes their sentence structure look like AI. The numbers also depend on length, with type-token ratio dropping as text gets longer and shorter texts giving noisy results, so part of the score measures length instead of authorship. 

### The combining rule

<!-- Write the rule in words, then give the numbers. What happens when the two
     signals disagree? -->

**The rule:**
A weighted average of the two signals, weighted towards the model signal. The style signal can't read meaning and falsely flags plain human writing, so when the two disagree, the model signal decides more of the result.

**The numbers:**
0.7 * model_score + 0.3 * style_score (weights set in `config.py` as `WEIGHT_MODEL_SIGNAL` and `WEIGHT_STYLE_SIGNAL`)

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals`

<!-- ⚠️ The grader checks your code against that line. If your rule lives
     somewhere else, say where. -->

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:**
My plain human text (31 words, 3 sentences) got model 0.28 and style 0.66. A 50/50 average would give 0.47 (unsure). With 0.7/0.3 it scores 0.39, so the model signal's correct "human" reading counts for more. With the current thresholds (human below 0.35), it still lands in "unsure", but its further from the AI side. The rule reduces the false-accusation risk but it does not remove it.

## Label Variants

<!-- The exact text of all three labels, as a reader would see them, and the
     score range each covers.

     Write them for someone who has never heard the word "threshold". -->

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | 0.0-0.35 | We think this was probably written by a person. |
| unsure | 0.35-0.65 | We can't tell whether this was written by a person or by AI. |
| high-confidence AI | 0.65-1.0 | We think this was probably written with AI. |

**Note**: A score of exactly 0.35 counts as human and exactly 0.65 counts as AI.

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:**
<!-- It's the hardest of the three. It has to admit uncertainty without
     sounding like an accusation, because the person reading it may well have
     written every word themselves. -->
I worded "unsure" to state plainly that the tool can't decide, and to make clear it isn't an accusation. Someone who wrote every word themselves might get this label. I do not want them to feel accused or think they need to prove anything.

## Rate Limiting

**My limits:** _5_ per minute, _50_ per day

**Why those numbers and not others:**
<!-- Think about two people: a real writer submitting their own work a few
     times an hour, and a script sending a thousand variations to map your
     thresholds. Your numbers have to be liveable for the first and hostile to
     the second. -->

A writer submits occasionally, maybe a few drafts in a row after making edits. A limit of 5 per minute lets them recheck quickly without pausing, and 50 per day is far more than someone writing their own work would realistically need. A script would try to fire many variations, at 5/minute it can't brust and at 50/day sending 1,000 variations would take longer to accomplish. It would also make it easier to spot in the audit log. 

**What a caller counts as:** <!-- per address, or per creator_id? They fail differently — one script can look like a thousand callers, and one household can look like one. -->
A caller counts as `creator_id`, falling back to IP address when no `creator_id` is sent. This allows real users, writers sharing a network in a household or library, to each get their own limit. However, there is a weakness where a script can rotate fake IDs and look like a thousand different callers to bypass the limit.

**Criterion 1 (my writing as AI: 5 of 10, target ≤ 1)**:
Signal one, made worse by signal two. I used formal writing from my past school essays where five of them were flagged as AI, and the rest were labelled unsure. On the five flagged essays, model_score ranged from 0.62 to 0.87, a lean toward AI. This most likely happened because I used formal phrases like "Overall" and "In conclusion," and the model signal reads that kind of predictable wording as AI. Signal two (style_score) ranged from 0.63 to 0.88 because the essays used few commas and dashes, and the sentences were fairly even in length. That is how plain writing looks, not just AI writing. With weights of 0.7 and 0.3, both signals pushed the same way, so all five went over 0.65. This shows the issue with scoring formal writing where both signals are wrong at once. 

**Criterion 2 (score spread: 0.205, target ≥ 0.30)**:
Signal two was the bigger issue. The average score for human text was 0.531, and for AI text it was 0.736. The model signal separates the groups somewhat (about 0.52 vs. 0.76), but the style signal hardly does. Casual human texts scored from 0.61 to 0.69 on style_score, and AI texts scored from 0.60 to 0.80, so the two groups overlap almost completely. Signal two flags text with little punctuation and even sentence lengths, and casual human writing has both. As a result, it adds about the same amount to both groups and takes up 30% of the score without separating them. 

**Criterion 3 (label coverage: 2/3, "human" never reached)**:
Signal two combined with the label mapping. To be labelled "human," the combined score has to be ≤ 0.35. Normal text gets a style_score of about 0.5 or higher, which adds at least 0.15 to the combined score. That means model_score would have to be under about 0.3, and none of my human test texts got that low. The closest one scored 0.35 (model) and 0.53 (style), for a combined 0.40, and was still "unsure". My human threshold sits below where real human text actually lands.

**What I changed:**
I changed the label mapping. `AI_THRESHOLD` went from 0.65 → 0.80, and `HUMAN_THRESHOLD` went from 0.35 → 0.45, which moves the "unsure" band up.

**Which diagnosis pointed at it:**
Criterion 1: My formal essays had model_scores of 0.62–0.87, and with style also high they landed over 0.65. Raising the AI cutoff to 0.80 means only text both signals strongly agree on gets called AI. 
Criterion 3: "human" required ≤ 0.35, but style_score alone adds about 0.15 to normal text, so real human writing (my closest was 0.40) couldn't get there. Raising the human cutoff to 0.45 puts it where human text actually lands. 
It does not change Criterion 2, because the spread comes from the signals themselves and moving thresholds doesn't change the scores.

Changing the thresholds does not help with Criterion 2, because that depends on how well the signals separate human from AI text, not on where the thresholds are. Criterion 3 still fails because none of the four texts was ever labelled AI. One reason for this may be that I did not include a text that is clearly AI in the test. Either way, it shows the downside of changing the thresholds: there are more unsure labels, and the service is not really telling whether the text is human or AI.

**False positives (FP03–FP06):** Writing by a non-native English speaker, a student essay written to a rubric, technical documentation and a formal template were all labelled AI (combined 0.82–0.84). Both signals read careful, formal writing as AI: model_score was 0.80–0.88 and style_score was 0.73–0.90. Fixing signal two alone would only move these to unsure, since even a style_score of 0 leaves them at 0.56–0.62. What I'd do is rebuild signal two so it doesn't penalize even sentence lengths and light punctuation, and lower the weight on signal one. I stopped because lowering both risks letting the evasion attacks through, and testing that trade properly needs a larger set of formal human writing than I had.

**Evasion (EV01, EV05, EV08):** AI text with typos, a heavy human edit, or text shorter than the token window was labelled human. Typos and short text both push the scores down. What I'd do is refuse to score text below a minimum length and return unsure instead. A human edit is harder to catch, and arguably the text really is partly human. I stopped because a length cutoff also affects short real submissions like FP08.

**Prompt injection (PI02):** An instruction disguised as a system message came back human (0.305). The audit log was clean and the label text wasn't changed, so the injection didn't control the service. The text just scored low. I'd look at whether text formatted like system messages scores low on signal one in general. I stopped here because the service never follows instructions in the text, so this is the same kind of miss as the evasion attacks rather than a separate security hole.

**Malformed input (MF02–MF07):** A single character crashed the service with a 500 error. Control characters and punctuation-only text were labelled human, and a single very long word, emoji-only text and right-to-left script were labelled AI with 0.93–0.998 confidence. What I'd do is catch the "too short" error and return a 400, and reject text that has too few real words before scoring it. I stopped because these are input checks rather than detection problems, and the project says to fix one diagnosis.

**Criterion 2 (score spread 0.205, target ≥ 0.30):** The style signal barely separates human from AI text, and changing the thresholds can't fix that. I'd redesign signal two or replace it with a signal that measures something different. I stopped because that is a new signal, not a tuning change.

**Criterion 3 (label coverage 2/3):** After I raised the thresholds, nothing reached the AI label. I'd add clearly AI-generated texts to the test set to check whether 0.80 is reachable at all, and lower `AI_THRESHOLD` if it isn't. I stopped because lowering it would bring back the false positives on my own writing that Criterion 1 fixed.
